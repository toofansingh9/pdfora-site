People usually reach for PDF to PowerPoint in one of two situations. Either they received a deck as a PDF and need to present or rework it, or they have a PDF report and want its pages as slides. Both are reasonable, but they lead to different expectations, and it helps to know what a converted presentation can and cannot give you.

A PDF page has no idea of slide titles, bullet levels, animations or speaker notes. A converter can only look at the page and recreate what it sees: text runs become text boxes, pictures become pictures, shapes become shapes, and each page becomes one slide. PDFora does this on a server through a conversion engine (CloudConvert, called via our relay), since a browser cannot rebuild PowerPoint objects with any reliability. Your PDF is uploaded encrypted, converted to .pptx and sent back, and the uploaded copy is deleted from the service after the job.

There are no settings: the whole PDF is converted, one file per job. Slide size follows the PDF page, so a deck originally exported from PowerPoint in 16:9 comes back in the same shape, while an A4 report becomes tall-ish slides that will need rethinking before they look good on a projector. Decks exported from presentation software usually convert best, because their pages were slides to begin with and their text was laid out in a few large blocks.

How to convert a PDF to PowerPoint

  1. Open PDF to PPT and choose a single PDF file.
  2. Click Convert. The file is uploaded and the status changes to Converting while the server works.
  3. Wait for the result; image-heavy decks take longest, and the tool stops waiting after about three minutes.
  4. Save the .pptx, which normally takes your PDF's name with the new extension.
  5. Open it in PowerPoint, Keynote, Google Slides or LibreOffice Impress and click into a few text boxes to see which parts are editable text and which are pictures.

When to use this tool

  • A conference speaker who only has the PDF handout of last year's talk and wants to update the figures.
  • School staff turning a PDF lesson plan into slides for the classroom projector.
  • Re-presenting a partner organisation's PDF briefing in your own team meeting.
  • Pulling charts and diagrams out of a report so they can be dropped into an existing deck.
  • Sales teams adapting a PDF product sheet into a short presentation for a client call.
  • Making a printed PDF poster editable when the designer's source file is unavailable.

What to know before you start

  • Conversion runs on a server: the PDF is uploaded encrypted and deleted from the conversion service after the job.
  • Each PDF page becomes one slide. There are no slide masters or layouts from the original deck; titles and body text come back as ordinary text boxes.
  • Animations, transitions, embedded video and speaker notes are not stored in a PDF, so none of them can be recovered.
  • Charts arrive as pictures or loose shapes, not as editable chart objects with data behind them. Rebuild a chart if you need to change its numbers.
  • Scanned pages or pages saved as images come back as a single picture per slide with no editable text. Running OCR PDF first gives the converter real text to work with, but do not expect the slide layout to come back perfectly.
  • Fonts missing from your computer are replaced on opening, which may make text overflow its box. Slide sorter view is the quickest way to spot the slides that came back as one flat image rather than separate, editable objects.

Tips for the best results

  • If the deck has many pages you do not need, extract the useful pages first; fewer slides convert faster and are easier to tidy.
  • Apply a slide master after converting: select all text boxes of one type and set a consistent font and size in one step.
  • Use PowerPoint's Selection Pane to see every object on a slide; converted slides often contain many small text boxes stacked on top of each other.
  • For A4 or Letter reports, consider placing the converted content onto fresh 16:9 slides rather than presenting the tall pages directly.
  • If you only need the slides as pictures, PDF to JPG or PDF to PNG is simpler and avoids layout surprises. Images keep their exact appearance, which is often all a handout needs.

Example: a school reusing a district training deck

A primary school's deputy head is asked to repeat a safeguarding training session for new staff. The district sent the original as a 24-page PDF exported from a presentation, and she needs to replace the contact names on four slides and add the school's own procedures.

She converts the PDF and opens the .pptx. Because the source was itself a 16:9 deck, the slide shape is right. Headings and bullet text are editable text boxes, so changing the names takes minutes. The flowchart on slide 9, though, comes back as a group of separate shapes and lines, and the district logo is an image. She ungroups the flowchart to change one box, leaves the logo alone and adds three new slides using her own layout.

The things she cannot get back are the original speaker notes and the build animations, because the PDF never contained them. She writes short notes for the new slides herself. The converted file saved her rebuilding 24 slides, and she spent her time on the parts that actually changed. A useful habit after any conversion is a quick pass in slide sorter view. It shows at a glance which slides came through with a real layout and which ones are a single flat picture, so you know before rehearsal where the editing time will go. In a typical exported deck most slides are fine and a handful, usually those built around a complex diagram or a screenshot, need manual attention. Mark those first, fix them, and leave the clean slides alone.

Troubleshooting

Every slide is a single picture
The PDF pages are images (a scan or a 'print to image' export). Text cannot be edited; you can only place new text on top or retype.
Text is broken into many little boxes
The PDF positioned text line by line. Copy the text out of the boxes into one new text box per slide and delete the fragments.
Slides are the wrong shape
Slide size follows the PDF page. Change it under Design > Slide Size and choose to scale or fit the content.
Text overflows its box
Your computer lacks the original font. Select the text and choose an installed font with similar width, or reduce the size slightly.
The job timed out
Large, image-rich PDFs can exceed the tool's waiting time of about three minutes. Split the PDF and convert it in parts, then combine the slides in PowerPoint.
Conversion failed on the server
The PDF may be protected or damaged. Open it in a viewer to check, remove protection with Unlock PDF or run Repair PDF, and retry.

Frequently asked questions

Will my animations and transitions come back?

No. A PDF does not store them, so they cannot be recovered. Add them again in PowerPoint.

Can I edit the charts?

Charts usually arrive as images or shapes without their data. Recreate the chart if the numbers must change.

Does it work with Google Slides or Keynote?

Yes. The output is a standard .pptx file that Google Slides, Keynote and LibreOffice Impress can open.

Are speaker notes included?

No. Notes are not part of a PDF page, so there is nothing to convert.

Can I convert several PDFs at once?

The tool takes one PDF per job. Convert them one after another, or merge them first if they belong in one deck.

Is my file uploaded?

Yes. This conversion runs on a server; the PDF is sent encrypted and removed from the conversion service after the job.
