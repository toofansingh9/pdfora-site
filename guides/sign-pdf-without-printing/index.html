How to Sign a PDF Without Printing It

By Toofan Singh · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

You can sign most everyday PDFs on screen by placing a drawn, typed or photographed signature with Fill & Sign, then checking and sending the -signed.pdf. That creates a signature image, not a certificate-based digital signature, so documents such as wills, court papers or filings that demand a qualified or certificate signature still need a proper signing service or ink.

Signing without a printer: what you actually need

The old routine was print, sign with a pen, scan or photograph the page, and email it back. It works, but it costs paper, produces a crooked grey scan, and is awkward on a phone. For most everyday paperwork you can skip all of it and place your signature on the PDF directly. What you need is the PDF itself, a way to add a signature (drawn, typed or from a picture of your real one), and a few minutes to check the result.

Before you start, it is worth being clear about what kind of signature you are making, because there are two very different things that both get called a digital signature. One is simply an image of your handwriting stamped onto a page. The other is a cryptographic signature backed by a certificate that proves who signed and whether the file changed afterwards. PDFora's Fill & Sign makes the first kind only. It does not create certificate-based digital signatures. The rest of this guide explains how to use it well and how to tell when you need something else.

How to sign a PDF on screen with Fill & Sign

  1. Open the file. Drop the PDF into Fill & Sign. Every page is drawn on screen, one below the other. The file is processed in your browser and not uploaded.
  2. Fill in the blanks first. Choose Text, pick a size before you click (10 to 12 suits most forms), click just above each printed line and type. Do this before signing so you can see how much space the signature line has left.
  3. Create the signature. Choose Signature and click near the line. In the window that opens, either draw on the pad with a mouse, finger or stylus, or switch to Type and write your name, which the tool sets in a script typeface from your own computer or phone.
  4. Place and size it. After Insert, move the signature onto its line and resize it with the handle in its corner. A new signature arrives no wider than 180 pixels; making it bigger is possible, but they are images and will blur if stretched a long way.
  5. Add the date and initials. Add a Text box for the date. For documents that ask for initials on every page, pick Signature again on each page, since every click starts a fresh pad.
  6. Save and check. Click Save PDF. The download keeps your original name with -signed.pdf added, so the unsigned file is not overwritten. Open it and look at every page before sending.

Drawn, typed or a photo of your real signature?

A drawn signature is closest to what you would do on paper and is the usual choice. The pad is 420 by 170 pixels and uses dark navy ink. A finger or stylus on a touch screen gives a smoother line than a mouse, and drawing slowly and fairly large helps it stay sharp when it is scaled down onto the line.

A typed signature is quick and perfectly readable, but it looks like what it is: your name in a script font. Because the script typeface comes from whatever is installed on your machine (Segoe Script on most Windows PCs, Brush Script on many Macs), it looks different on Windows and on a Mac. Some recipients are happy with this; others prefer something that resembles a handwritten mark.

An image of your real signature, for example signed on white paper and photographed, can be placed with the Image button in Fill & Sign, which shares its layer editor with Edit PDF. Crop the photo tightly and use a plain, evenly lit background, otherwise a grey rectangle shows around the ink. Keep that image file somewhere private: anyone who has it can paste your signature onto anything.

Electronic signature image vs digital signature

The difference matters more than the wording suggests, so here it is side by side.

Signature image (what Fill & Sign makes)Certificate-based digital signature
What it isA picture of a signature placed on the pageCryptographic data computed from the document with the signer's private key
Proves who signedNot by itself; context such as the email it came from does thatYes, through a certificate issued after identity checks
Detects later changesNo; the page can be edited afterwards without any warningYes; a PDF reader reports the signature as invalid if the file is altered
Shown in a PDF readerOnly as part of the pageIn a signature panel with signer and validity details
What you needA browserA certificate from a provider or an approved signing service

In the vocabulary of EU law, a signature image is a simple electronic signature: data attached to a document that shows the signer's intent. The European Commission's eSignature pages describe two further levels. An advanced signature must be uniquely linked to and capable of identifying the signer, and any later change to the signed data must be detectable. A qualified signature is an advanced signature created with a qualified device and based on a qualified certificate, and it is the only level the Commission describes as explicitly having the same legal effect as a handwritten signature throughout the EU.

When a signature image is usually accepted

Most countries do not require a particular technology for ordinary agreements. They care that the person intended to sign and that the record can be produced later. In the United States, the federal ESIGN Act says a signature or contract in a transaction affecting interstate or foreign commerce may not be denied legal effect solely because it is in electronic form. ESIGN also lets a state modify or replace that rule by enacting the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, so the law that applies to you may be your state's version of UETA rather than ESIGN alone. In the EU, the three-level system above exists precisely so that simpler signatures can be used where nothing stronger is required.

In practice, a pasted or drawn signature is commonly accepted for:

  • tenancy forms, inventories and renewal letters where the landlord or agent asks you to sign and email back;
  • school, club and volunteer consent forms;
  • freelance contracts, statements of work and purchase orders between businesses that already know each other;
  • HR onboarding forms, timesheets and expense claims;
  • delivery notes and simple acknowledgements of receipt.

The deciding factor is usually the recipient's own policy rather than the law. If the person asking for the document says a signed PDF by email is fine, a signature image is what they expect.

When you need a certificate-based signature or wet ink

Some documents are carved out of electronic signature laws or carry formal requirements that an image cannot meet. Examples from official sources:

  • United States: ESIGN's general rule does not apply to laws governing wills, codicils and testamentary trusts, adoption, divorce and other family law matters, court orders and official court documents, notices of utility cancellation, notices of default, foreclosure or eviction on a primary residence, cancellation of health or life insurance, product safety recalls, and documents that must travel with hazardous materials. State law may set its own rules for these.
  • European Union: where national law requires the equivalent of a handwritten signature, a qualified electronic signature is the level that has that effect across member states. A drawn image is not one.
  • India: the Information Technology Act gives legal recognition to signatures created with a Digital Signature Certificate issued by a Certifying Authority licensed by the Controller of Certifying Authorities, and later to approved electronic signature techniques such as the Aadhaar-based eSign service. The Act as passed in 2000 also listed documents it did not apply to, including negotiable instruments, powers of attorney, trusts, wills, and contracts for the sale or conveyance of immovable property. That list has been amended since, so check the current text before relying on it.

Beyond these, expect a certificate-based signature or ink when a portal or authority specifies one, when a document must be notarised or witnessed, for property deeds and land registry filings, and for filings with government systems that name a specific signature type. If a recipient tells you a signature is "not valid", ask exactly what they accept rather than trying again with a nicer-looking image. This section is general information, not legal advice; for anything with serious consequences, check with the recipient or a lawyer in your jurisdiction.

Flattening after signing

When Fill & Sign saves, everything you placed on a page, your text and your signature, is merged into a single image and stamped over that page. The original page content underneath is left unchanged. That means your signature is already part of the page: it cannot be dragged around in a normal PDF reader, and your typed answers are not searchable or copyable in the saved file.

What Fill & Sign does not do is remove interactive form fields. If the PDF had real fillable boxes, they are still live in the -signed.pdf, and anyone opening it can change their values. For forms like that, the clean sequence is:

  1. Type your answers into the form's own fields in your PDF reader and save. Some readers only store a value once you leave the field, so click elsewhere before you save.
  2. Open the saved copy in Fill & Sign, add only the signature and date, and save.
  3. Run the -signed.pdf through Flatten PDF, which draws each field's current appearance into the page and deletes the fields.
  4. Open the flattened file in a different viewer, ideally on a phone as well, and check that every answer and the signature appear.

Two limits to keep in mind. Flattening removes the editable boxes, yet the page itself can still be altered by someone using editing software, so it is no protection against deliberate tampering. And never flatten, compress or otherwise rewrite a file that already carries a certificate-based digital signature from someone else, because any change to the file makes that signature show as invalid. If your document needs both, do the PDFora steps first and apply the certificate signature last.

Before you send it

  • Check every page, not just the signature page; initials and dates are easy to miss.
  • Make sure the signature does not cover printed text such as the caption under the line.
  • Keep the unsigned original and the signed copy, named so that you cannot mix them up.
  • If the file contains personal details, consider adding an open password with Protect PDF and sending the password by a different channel, such as a text message.

Frequently asked questions

Is a drawn signature on a PDF legally binding?

Often, for ordinary agreements, because many laws do not reject a signature just for being electronic. Some documents are excluded or need a certificate-based or qualified signature, so check with the recipient if the stakes are high.

Does Fill & Sign create a digital signature with a certificate?

No. It places an image of your signature on the page. It does not add a cryptographic signature or show a signed-by panel in PDF readers.

Is my signature saved anywhere?

No. The signature exists only in the open browser tab and is gone when you close it, so you draw it again next time.

Can someone copy my signature from the signed PDF?

Yes, anyone with the file could screenshot or extract the image. That is true of any scanned or pasted signature, which is why it proves little on its own.

Can I sign a PDF that someone else already signed digitally?

Adding anything with PDFora rewrites the file and will make their certificate signature show as invalid. Ask the sender which signing service to use instead.

Should I flatten before or after adding my signature?

If the form has fillable fields, fill them, add your signature in Fill & Sign, then flatten the signed copy so the fields can no longer be changed.
