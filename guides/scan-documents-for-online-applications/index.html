How to Scan Documents for Online Applications With File Size Limits

By Toofan Singh · 31 October 2026 · 8 min read

Read the portal's rules first, then capture each page cropped and evenly lit at about 150 dpi (200 dpi for small print, 300 dpi for small cards like ID). Use grayscale unless colour is required, combine pages into an A4 PDF, compress it to your per-page budget, and zoom in to check every number before uploading.

Start with the form's rules, not the scanner

Application portals for jobs, university admissions, visas and government services almost always say exactly what they will accept, usually in a small line under the upload button or in a separate instructions PDF. Read it before you scan anything, because the rules decide every choice that follows. Write down four things:

  • Format. JPG only, PDF only, or either. Some portals want a photo and signature as JPG and the certificates as PDF on the same form.
  • Size range. A maximum, such as 100 KB, 500 KB or 2 MB, and sometimes a minimum as well. A minimum exists to stop people uploading thumbnails that nobody can read, so a file that is too small can be rejected just like one that is too big.
  • Pages. Whether a multi-page document (a marksheet with a back side, a three-page certificate) must go in as one file.
  • Colour and dimensions. Whether colour is required or grayscale is fine, and whether images must be a given size in pixels.

Limits differ between portals and change between application cycles, so treat the instructions for your specific form as the only authority. The numbers in this guide show how to work towards a limit; they are not a description of any particular portal.

Capturing the page: scanner or phone

A flatbed scanner gives the cleanest result: even light, no perspective distortion, and a resolution you choose. If you only have a phone, use a document-scanning mode rather than the ordinary camera, because it finds the page edges, straightens the perspective and crops away the table for you. On an iPhone, Notes and Files both have a Scan Documents option where you can drag the corners to fit the page (Apple). On Android, the Google Drive app has a scan button with crop, rotate, a grayscale filter and a choice of PDF or JPG output (Google).

Whatever you use, a few habits make the difference between a clean capture and one that gets rejected for being unreadable:

  • Put the document on a plain, dark surface so the edges stand out against it.
  • Use daylight from a window or a ceiling light rather than the flash. Laminated ID cards and glossy certificates throw back a white glare spot from a flash, and it nearly always lands on the number you need.
  • Hold the phone parallel to the page, directly above it. A tilted shot makes one end of the text smaller than the other.
  • Capture one document at a time. Leaving a second card or a bank statement at the edge of the frame sends someone else's details, or your own, to a stranger.
  • Flatten folded certificates under a book for a while first; a crease casts a shadow line across the text.

Choosing a resolution that fits the limit

Resolution is the main lever on file size. A scanner set in dpi (dots per inch) produces a fixed number of pixels for a given page, and the uncompressed data grows with the square of the setting: doubling the dpi quadruples the pixels. For an A4 page (8.27 × 11.69 inches) the arithmetic looks like this:

SettingPixels for A4Raw data, grayscaleRaw data, colourGood for
100 dpi827 × 1169about 0.97 MBabout 2.9 MBLarge, simple print only
150 dpi1240 × 1754about 2.2 MBabout 6.5 MBMost typed certificates and letters
200 dpi1654 × 2338about 3.9 MBabout 11.6 MBMarksheets and forms with small print
300 dpi2481 × 3507about 8.7 MBabout 26.1 MBArchiving; usually too much for upload

Raw data means one byte per pixel for grayscale and three for colour, before any compression. Saved as JPEG, the same page shrinks to a small fraction of that, and how small depends on the content: a white page with a few lines of text compresses far better than a certificate with a patterned, colourful border. That is why one page can come out at a few hundred kilobytes and the next at two megabytes from the same settings.

Small documents need a different way of thinking. An ID card is a fraction of the area of an A4 sheet, so it can be scanned at 300 dpi and still be a modest file. Measure your card: if it is 8.5 cm wide, that is 3.35 inches, which at 300 dpi gives about 1004 pixels across and about 638 down for a card 5.4 cm tall. That is enough to read the fine print of the card number and keeps the photo on the card recognisable. For full pages, 150 dpi is a sensible starting point and 200 dpi is worth it for marksheets where the grade table uses small type.

Grayscale, black and white, and when to keep colour

Grayscale stores one value per pixel where colour stores three, so the raw data drops to a third. For a typed certificate on white paper, colour adds nothing a reviewer needs, and grayscale is the easiest size saving you will make.

Keep colour when the form asks for it, and when the colour is the evidence. A coloured seal, a hologram on an ID card, a photograph of your face or a signature in blue ink can all be part of what an officer checks. When the rules do not say, a colour scan of the ID and grayscale for plain documents is a reasonable split.

Be careful with pure black-and-white mode, sometimes called text or 1-bit on scanners. It turns every pixel into either black or white, which gives tiny files but can erase faint stamps, light pencil marks and the photo on an ID card entirely. Grayscale keeps those as shades of grey. If you already have a colour PDF, Grayscale PDF converts it by redrawing every page at about 144 dpi in shades of grey; yellow highlighting and pale coloured stamps can turn very light, so check those parts afterwards.

Crop the image, not the PDF

A scan that includes the table, your fingers and a strip of carpet wastes pixels on things nobody needs, and those pixels cost file size. Crop tightly around the document in the scanning app or the phone's photo editor before you make the PDF.

Crop at the image stage for a technical reason too. Cropping a PDF page, including with Crop PDF, sets the visible area of the page but leaves everything outside it in the file. The file does not get smaller, and anyone with an editor can reveal the hidden edges again. For an identity document, that hidden strip might contain exactly the other card you meant to leave out.

JPG or PDF

When the form names a format, use it. When you have a choice:

  • Use JPG for a single image: a passport-style photograph, a signature, the front of an ID card. It is what most photo and signature fields expect.
  • Use PDF for anything with more than one page, or when the reviewer is likely to print it. A PDF keeps pages in order and can carry a standard page size.
  • Avoid PNG for photos. PNG is lossless, which suits screenshots but makes a photographed page much heavier than a JPEG of the same page.
  • Watch out for HEIC on iPhones. With the High Efficiency camera setting, iPhones save photos as HEIF/HEIC, which many portals and tools, including Images to PDF, do not accept. Setting Camera > Formats to Most Compatible makes new photos save as JPEG (Apple).

If you have a PDF and the portal wants a JPG, PDF to JPG turns each page into an image; the Screen setting gives the smallest files.

Combining pages into one PDF under a size cap

A size cap applies to the whole file, so first work out your budget per page. A 200 KB limit for a four-page document leaves about 50 KB per page; a 2 MB limit for ten pages leaves about 200 KB each. Then follow this order:

  1. Capture each page as a JPG, cropped and upright.
  2. Open Images to PDF, add the pages in order and set Page to A4 (or US Letter, if that is your paper). This matters: the default Fit to image size makes each page as large as the photo, about 4000 points wide for a 12-megapixel shot, and later tools that redraw pages at a set resolution would then produce enormous images.
  3. Download the PDF. It weighs roughly what the photos weighed together, because the images go in unchanged.
  4. If grayscale is acceptable, run Grayscale PDF on it now.
  5. Run Compress PDF at Recommended, which redraws each A4 page at about 124 dpi (about 1026 × 1450 pixels). If you are still over budget, try Strong, about 97 dpi (about 802 × 1134 pixels).
  6. If some documents are already PDFs, combine everything with Merge PDF and check the total size once more.

If a portal's minimum is the problem rather than its maximum, go the other way: compress at Low instead of Strong, or rescan at a higher dpi, instead of padding the file with blank pages.

Checking legibility before you upload

Portals check the format and the size; a person checks whether they can read it, often weeks later, and a rejection then can cost you the deadline. Before uploading:

  • Open the final file and zoom to 100%. Read every number aloud: roll numbers, dates of birth, certificate numbers, grades. These are what a reviewer compares against your form.
  • Look at the weakest parts: faint seals, signatures, the photo on an ID card, the smallest row of a marksheet.
  • Count the pages and make sure none is upside down or missing its back side.
  • Compare the size shown by your computer with the limit, and leave a small margin. Systems do not all count a kilobyte the same way (1,000 or 1,024 bytes), so a file at exactly 100 KB on your screen may be read as slightly over.
  • Give the file a plain name such as marksheet-class-12.pdf; some portals reject spaces or unusual characters in file names.
  • Keep a full-quality copy of every scan. If the reviewer asks for a clearer version, you can produce one in minutes instead of rescanning.

Frequently asked questions

What resolution should I scan certificates at for an upload form?

150 dpi is enough for most typed certificates and letters, and 200 dpi helps with small print such as grade tables. 300 dpi produces files that are usually larger than upload limits allow for full pages.

Why was my file rejected for being too small?

Some portals set a minimum size so that unreadable, low-resolution uploads are refused. Rescan at a higher resolution or use a lighter compression level rather than adding blank pages.

Can I upload a phone photo instead of a scan?

Usually yes, if the page is flat, evenly lit, cropped to its edges and readable at 100% zoom. A document-scanning mode in a phone app does the cropping and straightening for you.

Should I scan my ID card in colour?

Unless the form says grayscale is fine, colour is the safer choice for identity documents, because the photo, seals and security features are part of what gets checked. The small physical size keeps the file manageable.

How do I fit a multi-page document under a 200 KB limit?

Divide the limit by the number of pages to get a per-page budget, put the pages on A4 in Images to PDF, convert to grayscale if allowed, then compress. If the smallest text becomes unreadable, check whether the portal allows separate uploads per page.
