A slide deck is made to be presented. As soon as it has to be emailed, uploaded to a learning platform or printed as a handout, a PDF is easier on everyone: it opens on any phone, nobody needs PowerPoint, and the slides cannot be nudged out of place by a different screen size or a missing font. It also stops a recipient quietly editing your figures before passing the deck on.

This tool converts one .ppt or .pptx file. The presentation is uploaded over an encrypted connection to the CloudConvert service via PDFora's relay, rendered there by a presentation engine, and the PDF is downloaded back to you with the same name as the original. Your browser does not do the conversion, and the file is deleted from the service when the job finishes.

What you get is a static document: each slide becomes one page at the slide's own aspect ratio, usually 16:9. Anything that only happens while presenting, such as animations, transitions, embedded video or audio, and click-to-reveal builds, cannot exist on a page. Most of this page is about what to do with those parts before you convert, so the PDF still makes sense to someone who never saw you present.

How to convert PowerPoint to PDF

  1. Save and close the presentation. Delete or hide slides you do not want in the PDF.
  2. Open PowerPoint to PDF and choose the .pptx or .ppt file.
  3. Click Convert and wait while it uploads and converts. Decks with many large photos take longer.
  4. Download the PDF, which keeps the presentation's file name.
  5. Scroll through every page: check slides that relied on animations, and look for text that overflowed its box or changed font.

When to use this tool

  • Lecture slides posted to a school or university learning platform for students to read and annotate.
  • Sales and investor decks emailed after a meeting, so the recipient sees exactly what was shown.
  • Conference talks shared with organisers who publish speakers' slides as PDFs.
  • Training material printed for workshop participants.
  • Sending a deck to someone who only has a phone or a Chromebook.
  • Keeping a fixed record of a presentation as it was delivered on a particular date.

What to know before you start

  • Animations are flattened. A slide where bullet points appear one by one shows all of them at once in the PDF. Objects stacked on top of each other to be revealed in turn will overlap on the page.
  • Video and audio are not playable in the PDF. Usually only the poster frame of a video is kept. Put a link to the online version on the slide if the recipient needs it.
  • Speaker notes are not included; the converter produces slide pages. If notes matter, copy them into the slides or export a notes version from PowerPoint itself.
  • Hidden slides are generally left out of the PDF, which is a handy way to keep backup slides private, but check the page count against what you expect.
  • Fonts the server does not have are replaced, so a deck built with a special brand font can show different line breaks or text overflowing boxes. Embed fonts (File > Options > Save > Embed fonts in the file) or use common fonts.
  • Hyperlinks to websites usually remain clickable. Action buttons that jump between slides depend on how they were made and are worth testing.

Tips for the best results

  • For a reading copy, add a slide or two of context that you would normally say aloud; a PDF deck has no presenter.
  • Duplicate any build-up slide into separate slides showing each stage if the sequence matters to understanding.
  • Compress pictures in PowerPoint (Picture Format > Compress Pictures) before converting if the PDF must stay under an email limit.
  • Use a high-contrast theme if the PDF will be printed in black and white; dark slides waste toner and lose detail.
  • If you need several slides per printed page, convert first, then use N-up on the PDF to place two or four slides on each sheet.

A worked example: a teacher's lesson for absent students

A secondary-school science teacher wants to post a 28-slide lesson on photosynthesis for students who missed class. In the room, several slides build up step by step: a leaf diagram where labels fly in one at a time, and a slide where a wrong answer is shown and then covered by the correct one. There is also a two-minute video clip and the teacher's notes on what to say.

Converted as it is, the PDF shows the leaf diagram with all labels at once, which is fine, but the answer slide shows both answers stacked on top of each other, and the video is just a still frame. So she duplicates the answer slide and deletes the wrong answer from the copy, adds the video's web address as text under the still, and moves the two key points from her notes into a text box on the relevant slides.

The second conversion gives a 29-page PDF that a student can follow alone on a phone. Because the PDF keeps the 16:9 shape, it reads well on screen; for the few students who want paper, she prints it two slides per page with N-up.

One more check caught a problem she had not expected. A diagram of the chloroplast had been built from a dozen shapes grouped together, with one label typed in a decorative handwriting font she had downloaded. On the converted page, that label had been swapped for a plain font and now spilled past its arrow. Rather than embedding a font she was not sure she was licensed to share, she changed the label to the same font as the rest of the deck and converted once more. Checking each page against the original slide takes a few minutes, and it is the only reliable way to catch this kind of small substitution before thirty students see it.

Troubleshooting

Text on some slides overlaps or runs out of its box
A font was substituted with a wider one. Embed fonts in the presentation or switch to a common font, then convert again.
Several objects are stacked on top of each other
Those objects were revealed one after another by animations. Split the slide into several slides, one per stage, or remove the objects that were meant to disappear.
My video is missing
Media cannot play inside a PDF. Add a link to the video on the slide, or share the video separately.
Some slides are missing from the PDF
They are probably hidden slides. Unhide them in PowerPoint (right-click the thumbnail > Hide Slide) if they should be included.
Conversion failed or timed out
Very large decks with many high-resolution images can be slow, and damaged or password-protected files fail. Compress pictures, remove the password, save a fresh .pptx copy, and try again.
The PDF file is huge
Full-resolution photos are kept. Compress pictures in PowerPoint before converting, or run the PDF through Compress PDF.

Frequently asked questions

Is my presentation uploaded?

Yes, the file is sent encrypted to the conversion service and deleted after processing. Use PowerPoint's own export for decks that must stay on your computer.

Are speaker notes included?

No. Each slide becomes one page without its notes. Move important notes onto the slides before converting.

Do animations and transitions work in the PDF?

No. A PDF page is static, so every animated element is shown in its final position.

Will the PDF keep my slide size?

Yes. Pages take the presentation's slide size, so a 16:9 deck becomes wide pages and a 4:3 deck becomes squarer ones.

Can I convert Google Slides or Keynote files?

Download a Google Slides deck as .pptx, or export a Keynote presentation to PowerPoint format, then convert that file.

Can I put several slides on one page?

Not during conversion. Convert first, then use the N-up tool to place two or four slides on each page.
